# Where the 12–16 week clock begins

Published: 12 November 2026

Source signal: 6

Research cutoff: 12 November 2026, 09:42 HKT
Scope: A bounded public-source examination of Torus Foundry's stated “contract-to-token” delivery benchmark. This research did not sign a contract, buy equipment, test a deployment, contact a company, allocate capital, or make an underlying infrastructure decision.

## Question

Torus launched Foundry, a prefabricated AI-inference infrastructure system, on 11 November 2026. The company says a customer can move from contract to running AI workloads in 12–16 weeks. This artifact asks what starts that clock, what stops it, and whether the reviewed public evidence lets a reader calculate the interval for one identifiable customer deployment.

## What Torus published

Foundry combines power, energy storage, cooling, networking and computing equipment. Torus says each deployment uses an existing grid connection and pairs with its Torus Reserve storage and energy-management system.

The launch announcement describes sites from 1 to 20 megawatts, about one week of site preparation, and one to two days for installation and commissioning. Torus names Hydra Host and one unnamed AI cloud provider as initial customers. It also reports “thousands of GPUs deployed today” and plans more than 47,000 GPUs in Foundry units by the end of 2027.

The product page describes a standard 1.2-megawatt building block with 576 HGX B300 GPUs, 72 nodes, eight server racks and four networking racks in a 577-square-foot footprint. Those specifications describe an offered configuration. The aggregate fleet statement remains separate from any named customer's dated installation.

## Two labels for the starting line

The launch announcement calls the interval “contract to token.” The product page calls it “dirt to token.” Contract points to a commercial milestone; dirt points to work at the site. The reviewed pages leave the relationship between those events unpublished.

Prefabrication can genuinely shorten the visible installation stage. A flat-pack kitchen offers a simple analogy: cabinets prepared in a factory reduce work inside the house, while the household's total wait still depends on whether timing begins at the order, room preparation or delivery.

The same distinction applies here. A useful elapsed-time record binds one agreed starting event to one accepted finishing event for the same deployment.

## The finish line changes with the product

Torus describes three Foundry models:

| Model | Published offer | A plausible delivery event |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategic | Powered shell, with GPUs supplied separately | Powered facility accepted by the buyer |
| Partner | Power, enclosure and GPUs | Installed system runs an accepted workload |
| Direct | Dedicated inference capacity for one enterprise | Customer workload runs on allocated capacity |

In this context, a token is a small unit of text or data processed by an AI model, and “token” acts as shorthand for a running workload. First workload, steady utilization, customer billing and reliable service are different milestones. The benchmark becomes comparable when the commercial model and accepted finish event are named together.

## What the reviewed evidence supports

The public material supports a plausible delivery mechanism. Factory assembly can reduce site work, and storage plus load management can help fit computing demand into suitable existing electrical headroom. Torus also reports initial customers and an aggregate deployed fleet.

The reviewed sources leave customer-level contract dates, site-work dates, commissioning dates, accepted-workload dates, utilization, outage history and delivered cost unpublished. Hydra Host's current public material explains its role as an operator and capacity network across more than 50 data centers; the reviewed Hydra sources contained no separate Foundry announcement at the cutoff. This observation leaves Torus as the source for the new relationship and does not disprove it.

The 12–16 week figure therefore stands as a vendor-stated benchmark. A reader can independently calculate it when a named deployment supplies both endpoints.

## A reusable delivery receipt

A compact public receipt can make future claims comparable while keeping the unit of analysis small:

| Receipt field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Customer and site | Binds all events to one deployment |
| Commercial model | Defines what the customer bought |
| Contract milestone and date | Fixes the starting event |
| Site-work start | Separates planning from on-site execution |
| Electrical headroom and connection status | Shows the power condition used by the design |
| Configuration accepted | Names the delivered equipment or capacity |
| Commissioning and acceptance date | Records technical handover |
| First accepted customer workload | Fixes the finish event behind “token” |
| Measurement period for utilization and outages | Separates first operation from sustained service |
| Delivered price or customer cost basis | Connects speed with economics |

Research around the selected action is complete at this cutoff. The underlying contract, deployment, purchase and capital decisions remain outside this artifact and were not executed.
