# Twenty years is a duration, not a risk map

**Daily Special · 15 October 2026 · Signal 6**

## The bounded question

What do current public primary sources establish about who carries construction-delay, fuel, fuel-cell replacement and early-termination risk in the planned 702 MW AEP project serving Google near Cheyenne, Wyoming?

This card reads the announced project through four contract questions. It does not estimate a power price, value either company or recommend an investment. The underlying contracts are not public, so the map stops where the documents stop.

## What has actually been announced

American Electric Power said on 14 October that it plans to invest approximately **$4 billion** in a **702 MW** Bloom Energy fuel-cell facility. Google will buy the power under a **20-year PPA**. Cheyenne Fuel Cell, LLC is approximately **92% owned and controlled by AEP**, with nearly **8% held by funds advised by Basalt Infrastructure Partners**. Construction is planned to start in 2026 and full operation is expected by the end of 2028.

An AEP SEC filing from January gives the project an earlier contractual layer. An unregulated AEP subsidiary entered an **unconditional agreement to buy approximately $2.65 billion of solid-oxide fuel cells** and a 20-year offtake for **100% of project output**. At that stage the offtake still had conditions. AEP said it would be compensated for all capital and costs incurred if those conditions were not satisfied.

The project also sits inside Black Hills' service arrangement with Google. Black Hills says its contracts run through 2048 and that it will manage approximately **2.1 GW of third-party contracted resources** through a private microgrid, alongside up to 590 MW of grid-connected service. Black Hills says Google bears the costs of serving the planned data center under those agreements.

These layers matter. AEP owns and develops a generating asset. Bloom supplies and may service the fuel-cell equipment. Google buys power. Black Hills coordinates the wider service portfolio. A 20-year headline does not make those four roles interchangeable.

## Four risks, and how far the public map reaches

| Risk | What the public documents establish | What they do not reveal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Construction delay | AEP's equipment purchase was unconditional. The January offtake protected AEP's capital and costs if its then-pending conditions failed. AEP now plans a 2026 start and full operation by the end of 2028. Black Hills separately describes construction-period returns and contract protections in its Google service agreements. | The AEP PPA's milestone dates, delay damages, cure periods, relief events and which party pays under each trigger. |
| Fuel | AEP says the project will generate electricity from natural gas without combustion. Bloom says its systems need a constant qualifying fuel supply, typically from local gas utilities. Black Hills says Google bears costs associated with serving its planned data center. | Who procures gas for Cheyenne Fuel Cell, how commodity and transport costs flow through the AEP PPA, who bears curtailment, and who pays for the energy and infrastructure used by carbon capture. |
| Fuel-cell replacement | Bloom says its ordinary O&M service includes periodic fuel-cell replacement. Its standard product warranty generally lasts up to one year after operations begin, while extended maintenance is generally renewed annually. | Whether those ordinary terms apply here, the Cheyenne service term, performance guarantees, replacement schedule, caps, exclusions and responsibility after service expires. |
| Early termination | AEP disclosed protection for all incurred capital and costs if the January conditions were not met. Black Hills separately says its Google contracts include stranded-asset, early-termination and credit protection through 2048. | The AEP PPA's post-condition termination formula, collateral, redeployment rights, residual-value treatment and the party behind each payment. |

The Black Hills disclosures are useful, but they cannot be pasted into the AEP PPA. They show how the data-center service layer is protected. They do not publish the private terms between Google and Cheyenne Fuel Cell, or between AEP and Bloom.

## The mechanism: risk is layered, not erased

A long PPA improves financeability by giving the project a named buyer and a long revenue horizon. That is meaningful. It can make lenders and equity partners more willing to fund a capital-intensive asset.

But duration answers only one question: how long is the promised commercial relationship? It does not answer what happens when the plant is late, gas becomes expensive, stacks need replacement or the customer leaves early.

The public evidence supports a simple layered picture:

1. **Capital ownership:** AEP and Basalt-advised funds own the project company, so they fund and control the asset subject to their financing and contracts.
2. **Equipment performance:** Bloom's ordinary business model places some first-year warranty and renewable maintenance obligations with the supplier, but the project-specific package is not disclosed.
3. **Power purchase:** Google has committed to a 20-year purchase of project output, but the public announcement omits price adjustment and termination mechanics.
4. **Service integration:** Black Hills has separately disclosed cost pass-through, minimum-load economics, stranded-asset protection and credit support for the wider data-center service arrangement.

Risk can move between these layers through indemnities, pass-throughs, guarantees and collateral. The missing contracts determine whether it actually does.

## The four clauses that would complete the card

A decision-grade version would need only four short extracts, not another megawatt forecast:

- the commercial-operation deadline and delay-damages schedule;
- the fuel index, transport obligations and curtailment rule;
- the long-term service, performance and replacement obligations; and
- the early-termination payment, collateral and asset-redeployment terms.

Until those clauses are public, the defensible conclusion is narrow: the project has a credible buyer, a long term, named owners and a large committed equipment purchase. It does **not** yet have a publicly visible allocation for every risk that can change its economics.

## Method and limits

The selected action and vocabulary ranking were used only to discover the question. The four primary sources below were reopened on 15 October 2026. Project-specific claims come from AEP and Black Hills disclosures; Bloom's filing is used only to explain its ordinary fuel, warranty and maintenance model. No private contract, customer account, facility, fuel invoice or financing document was accessed. No trade, capital allocation, procurement, outreach or operating decision was made.
