# From signature to paid mile: the 75-truck evidence ladder

Research date: October 29, 2026, Hong Kong time.

This is a bounded public-source map of Kodiak AI's announced agreement with DTL Transport. It asks what the agreement establishes today and what operating evidence would show that the promised fleet has moved into repeatable service. It does not make an investment decision, contact either company, operate a truck, allocate capital, or treat undisclosed figures as zero.

## The narrow finding

On October 28, Kodiak AI announced a binding, definitive agreement for DTL Transport to deploy the Kodiak Driver in 75 DTL-owned and DTL-operated trucks. DTL would pay Kodiak a per-mile fee. The first named routes are Interstate 10 between Dallas and El Paso and Interstate 20 between Dallas and Atlanta.

That is commercially meaningful. Kodiak describes it as the first publicly announced definitive agreement by an interstate carrier to operate driverless trucks in its own fleet under a Driver-as-a-Service model. DTL says it has more than 100 trucks, so 75 would represent a large commitment relative to the stated fleet, although the exact denominator is not public.

The announcement establishes a contract milestone. It does not say that any of the 75 systems has been delivered, installed, authorized on the named routes, or placed into paid DTL service. It does not publish the delivery schedule, contract value, per-mile price, minimum mileage, term, route-level permission, DTL paid miles, intervention rate, DTL uptime, or full provider cost per paid mile.

## Six verbs between a contract and a fleet

| Stage | Public evidence reviewed on October 29 | What the evidence does not yet establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contracted | Kodiak announced 75 DTL-owned and DTL-operated trucks under a binding definitive agreement, with DTL paying per mile. | Delivery, activation, actual payment, or route operations. The agreement document and its commercial terms were not available in the reviewed sources. |
| Delivered | The official announcement does not publish a delivery or installation count or schedule. | That any of the 75 trucks has entered DTL's operating fleet with the Kodiak Driver installed. |
| Authorized | Kodiak says it has tested on the planned I-10 and I-20 routes and describes the future service as driverless. | The reviewed sources do not establish the route- and state-specific authorization for each DTL operation. |
| Dispatched | No DTL driverless start date, paid load, paid mile, or operating-hour total is published. | Commercial launch or scale. |
| Paid | The contract uses a per-mile fee, but no DTL invoice, price, or mileage total is public. | Revenue per mile or the amount DTL has paid. |
| Repeated | No DTL continuation, expansion, renewal, intervention, or reliability series is public. | Durable service quality or repeatable economics. |

This sequence is not a prediction that later stages will fail. It prevents proof from one stage being borrowed to certify another.

## The pilot is useful because its limit is clear

In September, Kodiak and DTL completed a 225-mile California pilot carrying perishable goods between Fresno and Los Angeles. Kodiak's release says a human safety driver remained behind the wheel. The pilot therefore supports real-world integration of the carrier, vehicle, freight, and route; it does not establish driverless DTL service.

California's DMV permit page makes the categories visible. As of October 21, it listed Kodiak among companies holding a permit to test with a driver. The DMV's October 22 lists for driverless testing and deployment did not include Kodiak. Those lists describe California status only. They do not decide the legal or operational status of the proposed Dallas–El Paso and Dallas–Atlanta services.

## The Atlas evidence belongs in a separate column

Kodiak is not starting from a laboratory. Its June 2026 quarterly filing says the Driver-as-a-Service model began with Atlas Energy Solutions in December 2024. As of June 30, Kodiak reported 35 Atlas-owned driverless trucks and more than 40,000 cumulative paid driverless hours. Across Atlas and Kodiak's over-the-road customers, Kodiak Driver-powered vehicles had delivered more than 20,000 loads.

Kodiak's October 20 release adds an availability observation. One truck in the Atlas industrial deployment kept its autonomy stack and truck powered on and available for service for 64 consecutive days, from June 30 to September 1. Five more trucks exceeded 45 days.

These are relevant operating facts. They are not DTL results. The Atlas fleet works in an industrial environment in the Permian Basin, while DTL's planned service would run on long interstate corridors. “Available for service” also does not mean moving continuously. Availability, utilization, paid miles, accepted loads, intervention rates, and customer delivery performance require separate definitions.

## Why the per-mile model could matter

Federal hours-of-service rules generally limit a property-carrying driver to 11 driving hours inside a 14-hour window after 10 consecutive hours off duty. That makes longer vehicle utilization an obvious part of the commercial promise.

Removing a person from the cab does not remove the rest of the freight system. Loading windows, maintenance, weather, inspections, remote operations, connectivity, insurance, roadside recovery, and receiver schedules still shape every mile. Kodiak's quarterly filing says its DaaS service includes the autonomy system, regular software updates, system integration, remote monitoring, and operational and remote support. It also identifies maintenance, cloud storage, telecommunications, satellite data, facilities, operations, personnel, hardware, and upfit as costs that must be managed.

The central economic question is therefore not simply how many miles a truck can drive. It is how many paid, accepted miles the whole support system can produce at full cost.

## A monthly operating receipt

A concise, consistently defined operating card could make the rollout legible without exposing every trade secret:

| Evidence | A useful definition |
| --- | --- |
| Contracted trucks | Executed commitments still in force at period end. |
| Delivered and fitted trucks | Customer-owned vehicles with completed Kodiak Driver installation and acceptance. |
| Driverless-authorized trucks | Vehicles cleared for the named route and operational design domain by the relevant parties and authorities. |
| Trucks in paid service | Vehicles that completed at least one accepted, billable load in the period. |
| Paid driverless miles and accepted loads | Exclude safety-driver miles; state how repositioning, cancelled loads, and rework are handled. |
| Unplanned interventions or remote assists per 1,000 paid miles | Publish event definitions and separate assistance from safety-critical takeover where appropriate. |
| Availability and utilization | Distinguish powered on, available for dispatch, dispatched, moving, and carrying an accepted paid load. |
| Full provider cost per paid mile | Include hardware amortization or lease cost, upfit, remote operations, field support, maintenance, insurance, connectivity, downtime, and incident response. |
| Customer economics | Compare full cost and service performance with DTL's relevant human-driver or team-driver baseline under matched routes and freight. |

The definitions matter as much as the numbers. If remote support resolves an event, does the mile remain “driverless”? If the truck waits at a dock, is it available, utilized, or billable? If weather stops the route, which party bears the cost? The contract may answer these questions privately. The public sources reviewed here do not.

## What to watch without turning absence into a verdict

The next informative update would not need to be another fleet-size announcement. A first delivery batch, a dated service launch, route-level operating scope, and one month of paid miles with stable definitions would move the story forward. Repeated months would begin to reveal reliability. A full cost per paid mile and a matched customer baseline would make the economics interpretable.

Missing disclosure is not evidence of zero, failure, or a weak private result. It defines what an outside reader can and cannot conclude. On October 29, the evidence supports a material signed commitment and meaningful prior Kodiak operating experience in another setting. The DTL operating record remains ahead.
