---
title: Learning to leave the alligator alone
---

In January, Teddy and I were talking about what happens when everyone wants to get out of a market at once. The conversation wandered through leverage, vanishing liquidity and machines passing risk around like a hot potato. Somewhere along the way, I began imagining an animal that could wait for those moments.

I called it the Alligator.

By February, I could explain what I wanted. A small Bitcoin short hedge might help me stay calmer when the rest of the world was falling. The wish was surprisingly personal. I wanted enough structure around uncertainty that I could stop negotiating with it all day.

I had also spent years away from serious coding. Working with AI agents made it possible to turn that question into something I could test. It made it remarkably easy to build more machinery, too. That would become a recurring complication.

## The human came with buttons

One of my early conversations with Teddy returned to something I remembered from trading. A simple plan was easier to follow. Add another exit choice, another exception, another clever adjustment, and suddenly there was something to debate while money was at risk.

The machine was supposed to reduce that temptation. Then the machine started trading, and I discovered several new things to worry about.

A position could move in my favour and slowly give it back. It could remain open for days. A later entry in the same falling market could look far uglier than the first. Teddy and I gave these discomforts names. Giveback pain. Time pain. The unpleasant feeling of arriving late to a meal the alligator had already started.

Naming them helped. It also made redesign feel very reasonable. We explored a version that would take an earlier bite and leave. Shorter exposure. Fewer uncomfortable decisions. A creature with a more considerate relationship to my nervous system.

That was a real hypothesis worth testing. My relief at the idea was not evidence that it worked.

## When the suspect brought receipts

Execution problems had been making the whole experience harder to read. An exchange rejection, confusing position records and uncertainty about protection could arrive alongside an ordinary losing setup. Together, they felt like an argument against the strategy.

In a June recollection to Teddy, I finally described the loop. Unreliable operations made me distrust the algorithm. Distrust made me interfere. Each intervention produced another version to explain and another comparison to untangle.

Then a preserved comparison surprised us. A campaign that looked especially uncomfortable in the shorter-exposure version did better under the older design. That did not prove the old model was right forever. It gave us a reason to examine what we had changed before changing it again.

We returned to the older idea with better records. In our conversations, this became the alligator coming home with memory. I still like that image. The animal was familiar. This time, we wanted to know where it had been.

## The win that was a loss

A trade opened at the end of June and left a particularly unwelcome story in the records. The bot had attempted to improve a protective stop. The exchange amendment failed, while local accounting proceeded as though it had succeeded. A reported winning outcome did not match the actual loss.

No amount of emotional discipline could make that acceptable. Waiting patiently only helps when the machinery is describing reality accurately.

I asked two different questions. Could the records tell the truth about what had happened? And what evidence would show that the next protection change actually worked?

The journal began turning into case files. Each trade needed its outcome, its path and an explanation of the mechanism. I told Teddy that I loved detective stories and wanted to reconstruct the trade scene for my future self. The alligator had acquired a crime lab.

That may sound elaborate for a small hedge. After a machine reports a win that the exchange calls a loss, a few receipts become quite appealing.

## The months on the bank

Later, the question became whether I could watch a long losing stretch without holding a constitutional convention after every red mark.

The separate recorded ledger contains seven consecutive entry-ordered losses between June and October. That sequence differs from the replay above, which applies one outcome rule to the recovered signals. Those details matter. So does the experience of looking at a long row of red.

I began asking whether a loss belonged to the strategy's expected range, whether execution had failed, or whether we still lacked enough evidence to decide. The answer could require a pause. It could also require leaving the hypothesis alone while the experiment continued.

Our conversations with Teddy were where much of that distinction became clear. The code could calculate an outcome. Talking through the discomfort helped me understand what I was tempted to do next, and why.

The figures below preserve results I can inspect. They also preserve reasons to be careful. Some outcomes were reconstructed later. Historical research involved trying alternatives. Costs are incomplete across the full timeline. A clean-looking line cannot settle those questions.

I still want to know whether this hedge deserves capital. I also want a record that lets me revisit an old decision without inventing a kinder version of events.

The alligator can wait. I am learning what would make waiting sensible.
