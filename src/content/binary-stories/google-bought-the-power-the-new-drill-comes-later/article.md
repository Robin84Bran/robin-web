Google has agreed to buy 50 megawatts of power from Quaise Energy's Project Obsidian in Oregon.

Quaise is known for a drilling system that uses millimeter waves to remove hard rock. It is tempting to read the agreement as Google's vote for that technology.

There is one awkward detail.

The first 50 megawatts will use conventional drilling. Quaise plans to use its new drilling system in a later 200-megawatt expansion.

Google bought the first power. The novel drill still has to earn its own receipt.

That does not make the agreement unimportant. It makes the agreement useful in a more precise way. The project now has a named buyer for a defined amount of electricity. The underground resource, generating plant and drilling technology still sit behind different evidence gates.

## One project, two engineering tests

Project Obsidian is designed as a 250-megawatt geothermal plant. Quaise divides it into two phases.

The first phase targets 50 megawatts using conventional drilling. Quaise says Google could begin buying that power as early as 2030 for its existing data-centre operations in The Dalles and for regional communities.

That phase is hardly easy. Developers must confirm the heat resource, drill and complete wells that survive extreme conditions, create dependable fluid circulation, build the generating plant and deliver power over time.

If it works, phase one will provide evidence for a commercial superhot geothermal system. It will say something about the site, the wells, the reservoir and the plant working together.

It will not yet say that millimeter-wave drilling can make a commercial deep well.

The proposed second phase adds 200 megawatts by going deeper and hotter. Quaise expects that expansion to become the first commercial use of its millimeter-wave system.

The sequence matters because a successful power plant and a successful drilling technology are not the same asset.

## The PPA de-risks one column

A power-purchase agreement gives an electricity project a buyer. That can help a developer coordinate financing, equipment, construction and grid work around a real commercial destination.

Google's agreement therefore strengthens Project Obsidian's route to market. It is better evidence than a general statement of interest.

The reviewed public sources do not disclose the price, contract duration, development conditions, guarantees, termination rights or allocation of delay risk. Those terms determine how much revenue certainty the agreement actually supplies.

So the PPA moves one column from “Who would buy the electricity?” towards an answer. It does not complete the columns for plant cost, well performance, delivery schedule or return on capital.

This distinction is especially important around AI infrastructure. A famous buyer can make an upstream technology feel finished long before the physical system has produced its first operating history.

The buyer is real. The delivery data are future evidence.

## The drill is only part of the well

Quaise's hybrid idea is elegant. Conventional rotary bits handle shallower sedimentary rock. A surface gyrotron then generates concentrated electromagnetic energy, a waveguide carries it downhole, and the energy melts or removes hard basement rock. Circulating gas carries the fine material back towards the surface.

The technology attacks a genuine bottleneck. The US Department of Energy says hard, fractured and abrasive geothermal rock slows drilling, while high temperatures limit downhole tools and materials.

But making rock disappear is not the same as completing an economic well.

The hole needs the right diameter and condition. Equipment needs useful availability. Energy use, maintenance, downtime and completion work all enter the bill. DOE says casing and cement can account for as much as half the cost of a geothermal well.

That creates a better diligence question than “How fast can the beam penetrate granite?”

The useful question is: what is the fully loaded cost of a durable, productive well?

Phase two is where that question becomes commercial.

## Four receipts, in order

Project Obsidian now has an evidence ladder.

1. **The PPA** establishes a named buyer and 50 megawatts of contracted capacity. Its undisclosed terms limit the economic conclusion.
2. **The confirmation work** tests what heat, pressure and permeability the site can actually provide.
3. **The first 50 megawatts** would show whether conventionally drilled wells and the generating plant can deliver dependable electricity.
4. **The 200-megawatt expansion** would test whether millimeter-wave drilling can create deeper wells reliably and at an attractive total cost.

Each receipt can improve the next financing conversation. None can substitute for the receipt after it.

This is the contrarian part of the Google announcement. The famous customer may help Quaise reach the test of its famous technology. It does not remove the test.

For investors, developers and electricity buyers, the sequence is more valuable than a single heroic headline. It tells us what became less uncertain and what did not.

Google bought the power.

The new drill comes later.
