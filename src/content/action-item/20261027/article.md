The most flattering thing that can happen to a new idea is an inbox full of complaints.

Someone hates the spreadsheet. Someone else loses Friday afternoons to copying numbers between systems. A third person has developed a private vocabulary for the software they are forced to use, none of it printable. Every message seems to say the same thing: *please build this*.

For a founder, especially one working alone, the temptation is almost irresistible. The pain is specific. The emotion is real. The sentences arrive pre-written for a pitch deck. It feels like demand.

It may be nothing more than weather.

## Pain is information

A complaint is useful because it tells you where friction lives. It can reveal a workflow nobody enjoys, a handoff that repeatedly fails, or a cost that has become socially acceptable only because everybody assumes it is unavoidable.

But pain does not automatically create a buyer. People complain about airport queues, passwords, tax forms and printer drivers every day. Most do not go looking for a vendor. They may tolerate the problem, delegate it, invent a workaround or decide that fixing it would be more disruptive than living with it.

The missing question is simple: what has the person already spent?

Not only money. Time counts. So does assembling a real case, arranging access to colleagues, explaining the current workaround, exposing an ugly internal process, comparing two proposals or taking a concrete price to a budget owner. These acts cost something. They turn a description of pain into evidence of intent.

That is why the distance between a complaint and a customer is best measured in sacrifices.

## A prototype can flatter you too

Proof Market, a local open-source prototype built this week, is designed to take an incoming business enquiry through routing, follow-up, human approval, a mock offer and a buyer-owned evaluation. It can preserve evidence, compare independently run implementations and record a willingness-to-pay response.

The code works. The repository is public. The workflow can be demonstrated.

None of those facts creates a customer.

The project's own README is unusually blunt about this. A demo click, a mock acceptance, an agent test and a published repository are all excluded from demand validation. Its proposed first value test starts somewhere less comfortable: one actual SME buyer supplies a current workflow, a baseline and anonymized cases. At least two candidate implementations run against those cases. The buyer inspects the results, makes a pilot decision and responds to a concrete scoped price. A refusal counts.

That final sentence is the important one. A polite “interesting” preserves everybody's options. A refusal to a specific offer closes one. It tells you where the value, timing, trust or price failed to clear a real threshold.

A no can be more generous than applause.

## The ladder gets heavier

Imagine a ladder whose rungs require progressively more from the potential buyer.

On the first rung, someone complains. You learn the language of the problem.

On the second, they describe what they do today. Now you can see whether the problem is frequent, expensive or merely irritating.

On the third, they provide one of their own cases. This costs them time and introduces reputational risk: the case might expose messy data or an awkward process.

On the fourth, they help compare your proposal with the current workaround. The conversation has moved from fantasy to substitution.

On the fifth, they accept or reject a bounded pilot with named inputs, duration, success conditions and price. The buyer has finally met the idea as a commercial object.

Only later come payment, successful delivery, repeat use and expansion. Each rung makes the evidence more expensive and therefore harder to fake accidentally.

This is also why a large enterprise announcement can mean more than a polished demonstration without proving the whole business. On October 26, GXO said it planned to extend Agility Robotics' Digit to two more US sites in early 2027. The company reports more than 100,000 totes moved at an existing site and more than 65,000 hours in real-world operations. Expansion by the same customer is a stronger signal than a stage demo because someone with operating experience has chosen to continue.

Yet the announcement does not disclose price, intervention rates, downtime, payback or profit. Repeat buying raises the quality of the question. It does not answer every question.

## Fourteen days without pretending

For a one-person AI company, the useful experiment does not need a campaign, a CRM migration or a new automated outreach machine. It can fit on one page and end in fourteen days.

Choose a small set of complaints from people already within an authorized research boundary. Write down the hypothesis before asking for anything: *this problem is costly enough that a buyer will spend effort evaluating a bounded alternative*.

Then ask for progressively heavier evidence. Can the person describe the current workaround and its time cost? Will they supply one anonymized real case? Will they compare two possible implementations using the same case? Will they take a scoped pilot with explicit exclusions and a concrete price to whoever can decide?

The experiment should record the last rung reached, not convert every conversation into a lead. It should preserve refusal language without turning it into failure. “The problem is real, but procurement would take six months” is different from “we would never pay for this” and different again from “send the pilot terms on Thursday.”

The continue rule can be set in advance: proceed only if at least one real buyer contributes a case, evaluates alternatives and gives a concrete pilot and price response. Stop or redesign if the evidence remains at complaints and compliments. Keep missing costs as unknown rather than zero.

Nothing in that design requires pretending the test has already happened. Today it has not. No real Proof Market buyer, paid pilot or revenue has been observed. The experiment is a way to earn the next fact.

## The doorway

Founders often talk about listening to customers. The phrase makes the work sound acoustic, as if the answer is hiding in the right interview question.

Listening matters. It teaches you the map of the pain. But the border of a market appears somewhere else: at the moment a person places something scarce on the table.

It might be an afternoon. It might be a messy file. It might be permission to involve a colleague, a signed pilot or a budget. The object changes; the crossing does not.

A complaint happens outside the doorway. A customer begins to appear when someone steps through.
