# The $6.5 Billion Lease That Never Reached Closing

Before an artificial-intelligence service can answer a question or generate an image, computers somewhere have to do the work. Those computers need buildings, cooling and a dependable supply of electricity. Creating that physical foundation takes land, construction and money, often committed well before a customer begins using the facility.

Fermi Inc., operating as Fermi America, is developing one such foundation near Amarillo, Texas. Its Project Matador is a planned energy and data-center campus. In August 2026, Fermi announced a customer agreement large enough to give the project a striking headline: approximately $6.5 billion in contracted revenue over 15 years.

The customer was TensorWave, a company that supplies computing capacity for AI. Fermi would provide a complete data-center facility, and TensorWave would become its tenant. AMD agreed to provide associated credit support—a financial arrangement intended to give greater assurance that the customer’s obligations would be met.

Together, these commitments appeared to connect a developer, a customer and financial backing around a substantial construction project. The next three months showed how much depended on the steps between signing the agreement and closing it.

## A signature with conditions attached

On August 10, Fermi announced what it called a “binding lease agreement” with TensorWave. The first phase contemplated a turnkey data center with 222 megawatts of capacity, beginning in the second half of 2027. Turnkey means the developer delivers a more complete facility for the customer to use.

A megawatt, abbreviated MW, is a measure of power. Here, 222MW described the planned electrical scale of the first phase. Construction progress, server operation and revenue each require their own evidence.

Fermi put the first phase’s total contracted revenue at approximately $6.5 billion over 15 years. That figure represented the revenue contemplated across the life of the agreement. Cash would depend on the contract’s payment terms and performance. Fermi also said the parties intended to expand their partnership to more than 650MW across three phases.

For a developer preparing a large campus, a long-term customer agreement can help support the case for construction financing. Lenders need to understand how the project will earn money and who stands behind the promised payments. The August announcement therefore mattered for more than the size of its headline.

It also described financing and credit-support conditions. Those conditions were part of the deal.

A familiar illustration is a house purchase. A buyer and seller can sign a binding agreement while the purchase still depends on a mortgage and other agreed conditions. Closing comes when the required conditions have been satisfied and the transaction reaches its agreed completion point. The signature establishes obligations; the route to closing runs through the terms of the contract.

A large data-center lease can follow a similar sequence. Calling it binding describes the legal agreement that has been signed. Its closing provisions determine what must happen next.

## The deadline that passed

The parties continued to revise the TensorWave agreement after the August announcement. According to Fermi’s quarterly filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, they amended it on September 22, then amended and restated it on October 9.

The revised terms allowed an October 31 closing date to be extended as far as December 31. That gave the parties a contractual route to more time.

October 31 passed, and the closing date remained unextended. On November 10, the lease terminated in accordance with its terms. Fermi’s subsequent release said the associated AMD credit-support agreement terminated alongside it.

The public filing establishes that sequence. The parties’ reasons for allowing the deadline to pass remain private in the reviewed record. Financing and credit support were meaningful parts of the agreement; identifying either as the decisive cause of termination would go beyond the disclosed facts.

Fermi also stated that the termination left its unaudited condensed consolidated financial statements for the periods ended September 30, 2026, unaffected. That statement concerns the financial reporting periods specified in the filing. The commercial outcome was clear: the signed TensorWave lease reached termination before closing.

The August announcement and the November filing can therefore be read together. In August, Fermi had a signed agreement, binding under its terms, with a large multiyear revenue figure. By November, that agreement had ended through the process those terms provided.

The intervening conditions explain how both descriptions can be accurate.

## A different customer and a different job

November 10 also brought a new direction for Project Matador.

That day, Fermi entered a nonbinding letter of intent with a prospective replacement customer for approximately 600MW of powered land. A letter of intent sets out a proposed transaction; the filing expressly identifies this one as nonbinding.

Fermi described the customer as an investment-grade hyperscaler and kept its identity confidential. A hyperscaler operates computing infrastructure on a very large scale. Investment-grade credit generally signals an assessed capacity to meet financial obligations, which matters to developers and lenders considering a long-term project.

The proposed arrangement also changed what Fermi would deliver.

Under the TensorWave agreement, the first phase called for a turnkey data center. Powered land instead provides a site with power and development work prepared for the customer’s data-center build. The customer takes on more of the facility construction.

That difference makes the jump from 222MW to approximately 600MW easy to misread. The figures describe the electrical scale of two different arrangements. Each has its own division of construction work, funding needs and delivery obligations. A larger powered-land commitment therefore requires its own economic assessment.

Alongside the letter of intent, Fermi and the prospective customer entered a $200 million prepayment agreement and a $15 million early-construction and site-development agreement.

In its November 12 release, Fermi presented these as financial and development commitments being made ahead of a definitive long-term lease. The company said the parties were working toward that lease. It also described a contemplated $90 million structural-steel and building-materials purchase agreement, with finalization still under way.

These distinctions matter because the replacement relationship had several parts at different stages. The prepayment and early-development agreements had been entered into. The underlying letter of intent was nonbinding. The definitive long-term lease remained a work in progress in the reviewed disclosures, and the materials purchase agreement remained contemplated.

Fermi described the customer’s commitments as more than $200 million up front. The reviewed documents establish the agreements while leaving the cash-receipt date and the funds’ unrestricted availability unresolved. The complete refund, security and other financial protections also remain questions for the definitive terms and further disclosure.

In the same release, Fermi described itself as pre-revenue and pre-lease at the time of the new customer agreements. That was the company’s stated position as it began this next phase.

## What makes a promise financeable?

The replacement structure suggests a possible shift in who supplies money early and who carries responsibility for building the facility. Customer prepayments and customer-led construction may change the developer’s financing burden. How far that shift goes depends on the actual payment terms, protections and delivery obligations.

For a lender, those details determine how confidently future customer payments can support money advanced today. A prepayment’s usefulness depends partly on when it arrives and the conditions governing its use or return. Credit support matters through the obligations it covers. A delivery promise matters through the work and spending required to fulfil it.

The Fermi chronology gives those otherwise abstract distinctions a concrete shape. Announced, signed, closed and operating are four separate states. The TensorWave agreement reached signing and later terminated before closing. The replacement relationship introduced signed preliminary financial and development agreements while its definitive lease remained under negotiation. Operating capacity belongs to a later stage, established through completed facilities and actual use.

The $6.5 billion headline described the scale of a signed promise. The subsequent filings showed the conditions governing its survival. The new arrangement brought a different customer profile and a different allocation of work, with its final economics still emerging.

As Project Matador moves forward, the documents leave a natural question: which condition actually turns a signed promise into financeable infrastructure?
