# If SpaceX Buys Tesla, Who Gains a Vote?

SpaceX and Tesla work on different parts of the future Elon Musk wants to build. SpaceX operates launch, satellite, communications and AI-related businesses. Tesla makes electric vehicles, energy products and robotics systems. Musk serves as chief executive of both, linking two companies whose shareholders have separate financial interests and different powers over corporate decisions.

A combination would bring those differences into one negotiation. Tesla shareholders would have an interest in the value offered for their company. SpaceX shareholders would have an interest in what their company gives up to acquire it. If payment included SpaceX shares, the transaction would also distribute something with lasting importance: votes.

That makes this a story about how ownership works. A share gives its holder an economic stake in a business. Its voting rights help determine how much say that holder has in decisions put to shareholders, including choosing directors. At SpaceX, the share class makes a large difference to that say.

The possibility of a combination has now reached formal board review. As of November 25, 2026, Hong Kong time, the public record describes an exploratory process. Whether the companies reach an agreement, what its terms might be and whether a transaction would close remain open.

To understand what shareholders could gain, it helps to start with the voting arrangements already described in the companies’ filings.

SpaceX’s amended prospectus, dated June 1, 2026, sets out two classes of voting common stock. Each Class A share carries one vote. Each Class B share carries ten. This arrangement is called a dual-class share structure: ownership comes in two forms with different voting strength.

Class B also carries a separate power. While any Class B shares remain outstanding, their holders, voting as a group, elect 51% of SpaceX’s board. That gives the class a route to choosing a board majority alongside its greater voting weight. The prospectus also describes transfer rules: Class B shares generally convert into Class A when transferred outside defined permitted transfers.

For an illustration, imagine a club that issues memberships. Each membership gives its owner a stake in the club, while one kind comes with a single ballot and another comes with ten. The ten-ballot members also choose just over half the club’s governing committee. Counting memberships tells you something about ownership. Counting ballots and reading the committee-election rules tells you who can steer the club.

SpaceX’s prospectus provides a dated picture of how concentrated those votes were. Its beneficial-ownership table reports that Musk held 85.1% of combined voting power as of May 1, 2026, before the offering. That is a pre-offering snapshot, with its own date and circumstances.

Tesla’s 2026 proxy provides a different kind of snapshot. Using September 30 share data, its beneficial-ownership table reports Musk with 699,580,882 beneficially owned shares and a 19.8% ownership figure. “Beneficial ownership” is a filing category used to identify holdings attributed to a person under disclosure rules; the table and its footnotes explain what the figure covers. Tesla’s footnotes place an additional 423,743,904 restricted shares from Musk’s 2025 CEO award outside his voting control in that table because they are subject to a voting agreement.

The two headline percentages therefore describe different things at different times: SpaceX’s pre-offering combined voting power and Tesla’s later beneficial-ownership figure. Each helps explain the starting position. Calculating control after a future combination would require the proposed transaction’s terms and ownership information on a consistent basis.

Against that background, the companies’ quarterly filings brought the possible combination into view.

In its Form 10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, SpaceX said its board had formed a special committee to evaluate a potential combination with Tesla. Tesla’s corresponding filing said its board had formed a special committee of disinterested directors to explore a deeper relationship with SpaceX, potentially including a business combination.

These committees matter because Musk occupies roles on both sides. A buyer’s shareholders generally benefit from obtaining a business on attractive terms; a seller’s shareholders generally benefit from receiving attractive value. A shared chief executive creates a related-party conflict for the boards to manage. Separate committees provide a process for examining the interests of each company’s shareholders. The fairness of any eventual terms and the outcome of the negotiations remain questions for that process.

On November 24, the *Financial Times* reported that three Tesla directors and two SpaceX directors were serving on the negotiating committees. Reuters repeated the three-and-two count, attributing it to the *Financial Times*. The distinction is useful: the companies’ filings establish that the reviews exist, while the committee membership details come from reporting.

The filings also describe approval requirements. SpaceX says a combination would require further approval from its special committee, its board and its controlling shareholder. Tesla says a transaction would require its special committee’s affirmative recommendation and any necessary shareholder approval.

So the title’s “if” carries real weight. As of the November 25 cutoff, the purchase price, payment mix, exchange ratio and any share class used as payment remain unresolved in the reviewed public record. An agreement and a completed transaction remain uncertain.

That brings the story to a practical question: if Tesla shareholders received SpaceX shares, how much ownership—and how much say—would arrive together?

A deal paid in shares changes two tallies at once. One records economic ownership: how much of the resulting business each holder owns. The other records voting power: how many votes each holder can cast and which election rights those shares carry.

Consider a simple arithmetic illustration. Hold the number of newly issued SpaceX shares constant at one billion. If those shares were Class A, they would add one billion votes. If they were Class B, they would add ten billion votes. This is an illustration of the existing voting rules; it is separate from the companies’ negotiations and any prediction about their terms.

The recipients would matter just as much. If shares were distributed proportionally among Tesla holders, each holder’s allocation would follow the exchange ratio—the number of buyer shares received for each Tesla share. The voting power arriving with that allocation would depend on the class received and its applicable rules.

Musk’s holdings make this especially relevant. As a holder in both companies, he could have an existing stake on the buyer’s side and receive consideration for eligible holdings on the seller’s side. Other investors could also own shares in both. A new share issuance expands the total pool, while recipients may add to holdings they already possess. The final effect on any person’s control depends on both changes.

This is why a purchase price would tell only part of the story. The price describes the value offered. The share count, exchange ratio, voting class, conversion rules and recipients would explain how a share-based payment redistributes ownership and influence. The approval requirements would establish whose consent the proposal needs.

For now, the concrete development is a formal review by two boards, followed by reported details about their negotiating committees. A future proposal would turn the broad possibility into terms shareholders could examine.

When those terms arrive, who gains a vote will depend on a few plain questions: which class is issued, how many shares are issued, who receives them, and whose approval is required?
