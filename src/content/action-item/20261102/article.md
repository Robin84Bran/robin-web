# Intel's AI Boom and Stock Crash Can Coexist

Intel makes the chips that help computers do their work. It designs and manufactures semiconductors, including processors used in personal computers and the servers that power large computing systems. As spending on artificial intelligence grows, Intel’s business supplying data centers has become an important part of its story.

That business sits inside a larger company. Intel also serves the personal computer market and operates manufacturing facilities. Its shareholders own a stake in the combined enterprise, with its different customers, expenses and prospects. The fortunes of one division help shape the whole, while the other businesses continue to matter.

On October 29, 2026, Intel released results showing remarkable growth in its Data Center and AI division. The next day, its shares fell 28.58 percent.

Those two events raise an understandable question: how can a company report booming sales in such an important business and then lose more than a quarter of its share price?

The answer begins with what each figure measures—and which period it describes.

## A strong quarter with different stories inside it

Intel’s October 29 earnings release covered its third quarter, which ended September 26, 2026. Total revenue reached $16.5 billion, up 21 percent from the same period a year earlier. Revenue is the money a company records from sales before expenses.

Within that result, the Data Center and AI segment, usually shortened to DCAI, reported $7.3 billion in revenue, up 78 percent from a year earlier. A segment is an operating part of a larger company. DCAI sells processors and related products used in servers and AI infrastructure.

Intel described demand for Xeon, its family of server processors, as extraordinary. Management said unlocking additional supply was a near-term priority. That combination matters: customers can want more chips while the company works to increase the quantity available to sell. Demand and delivered sales move together only as production and supply allow.

Intel’s detailed quarterly filing added another piece of the growth story. “Other DCAI” revenue reached $1.4 billion, an increase of $743 million from a year earlier. Intel attributed that increase to higher demand for purpose-built silicon—chips designed for particular tasks. This category sits within DCAI, so it adds detail to the division’s reported performance.

Elsewhere in the company, the direction differed. Intel’s Client Computing and Physical AI Group reported revenue of $8.1 billion, down 5 percent from a year earlier. Intel also cited near-term headwinds in the personal computer industry.

The figures therefore described a company with businesses moving at different speeds. Server and AI-related sales were growing rapidly. Another large business was recording lower sales.

There is also an accounting detail worth keeping with those numbers. Intel says its Intel Products segment revenues include transactions between segments and are rounded. They serve as measures of business performance; the consolidated company total accounts for the relationships between those businesses.

## The forecast looked ahead to a lower total

Alongside its completed third-quarter results, Intel offered guidance for the fourth quarter. Guidance is management’s forecast of what the company expects to achieve.

Intel projected total fourth-quarter revenue of $15.0 billion to $16.0 billion. Even the upper end of that range was below the $16.5 billion recorded in the third quarter.

Two comparisons were now in view. The 78 percent DCAI growth figure compared one division’s third-quarter sales with its sales a year earlier. The fourth-quarter forecast compared the expected next quarter for the whole company with the quarter just completed.

Both could be accurate at the same time.

The forecast’s scope is essential. It covered Intel as a whole. DCAI’s own fourth-quarter direction remained an open question within that group forecast. Readers could see management expecting a lower company revenue total while also describing exceptional demand for server processors.

Consider a department store as an analogy. Its electronics floor has a surge in sales, while another large floor sells less than it did a year earlier. The owner expects the entire store to bring in less money next quarter than it did this quarter. The electronics floor’s success remains real, and the storewide forecast remains relevant to anyone buying a share of the business.

Intel’s reported figures have that same part-to-whole relationship. Its manufacturing operations and product supply add further considerations: converting customer demand into revenue takes available chips, and converting revenue into financial returns involves expenses and investment.

## The market put a new price on the whole company

On October 30, the trading day after the earnings release, Intel’s shares closed sharply lower.

StockAnalysis records Intel’s adjusted closing price at $92.07 on October 29 and $65.76 on October 30, a one-day decline of 28.58 percent. The service attributes its historical adjusted prices to S&P Global Market Intelligence.

The broader market also fell. QQQ, an exchange-traded fund that tracks the Nasdaq-100 index, declined from an adjusted close of $715.34 to $690.49 over the same dates, a fall of 3.47 percent.

That comparison gives the Intel move useful context. Shares across the Nasdaq-100 were under pressure, and Intel’s decline was much larger. The day brought a sharp repricing of Intel relative to that broader benchmark.

The price record establishes the size and timing of the move. Explaining precisely how investors arrived at it requires more interpretation.

A share price reflects what buyers and sellers will accept today for a stake in a company’s future. Earnings reports bring fresh information into that judgment. Investors can weigh recent sales, the next quarter’s forecast, product supply, expenses, financing and the price they were already paying for anticipated growth.

A strong reported result can therefore accompany a falling share price when the overall outlook falls short of expectations already embedded in the shares. That is a general mechanism that helps explain how these events can coexist. For Intel’s October 30 decline, the available sources leave the contribution of each possible factor unresolved. Guidance, supply conditions, the PC market, valuation, investor positioning and the wider market could all have played a role.

## From growing sales to lasting value

DCAI’s 78 percent growth establishes a substantial increase in that division’s revenue. Understanding the financial benefit to Intel requires following those sales further through the business.

Revenue counts sales before expenses. Operating cash flow measures cash generated by operations. Free cash flow commonly measures what remains after capital spending, such as investment in manufacturing equipment. Each answers a different question about the same company.

For a chipmaker, that distinction has practical weight. Selling more processors can expand the business while producing and funding that expansion also consumes resources. The durability of the benefit depends on what Intel earns from those sales, how much cash operations generate and the investment required to sustain them.

Intel’s third quarter showed rapid DCAI growth and higher total revenue than a year earlier. Its fourth-quarter guidance pointed to a lower company revenue total than the quarter just completed. The following day’s trading placed a substantially lower price on the shares.

The story leaves a question larger than any single growth figure: how much of Intel’s rising data-center demand will become lasting cash generation for the whole company, and how much success had shareholders already priced in?
