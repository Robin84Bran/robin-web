# When the App Becomes a Conversation

An app gives a task a visible shape. When you order food, a menu shows the choices, a cart holds your selections, and a checkout screen brings the price and delivery details together. A confirmation then tells you the order has gone through. Each screen helps answer a simple question: where am I in this process?

A conversation can bring that whole journey into a single message thread. You describe what you want, answer a few questions, approve the purchase and receive the result in the same place. The words carry both the conversation and the instructions that move the task forward.

The software making this possible is often called an **AI agent**: a program that can interpret a request, ask follow-up questions and use other software to carry out a task. Its communication channel is simply the route through which you speak to it, such as iMessage, SMS or WhatsApp. The channel carries the exchange; the agent works on the request; a business supplies the goods or service. You remain the person whose intentions and approval guide the action.

This is the setting for Linq’s latest funding announcement. Linq supplies infrastructure that businesses can use to put agents into messaging services and other computing environments. Its ambitions offer a view of a larger change in software: the conversation is becoming a place where people can both discuss an action and authorize it.

Three everyday ideas help explain what that change involves. **Explicit consent** is a clear approval of a particular action, tied to its price or other consequence. A **completion receipt** is a record of what happened, when it happened and what money or other resource was involved, together with a route for help or reversal. **Recovery** is the practical way to clarify, cancel, correct or address an outcome, sometimes with a person’s help. These are design concepts for understanding the shift. Their implementation depends on the product and the task.

On November 18, 2026, Linq announced $90 million in new funding led by Andreessen Horowitz. TQ Ventures, Mucker Capital, Abstract, Anthos and Left Lane Capital also participated. Linq said the money would support messaging, computer use, voice calling, software and hardware infrastructure, and partnerships.

Messaging was the company’s first product. Linq says its platform supports customer-facing agents through iMessage, RCS, SMS and WhatsApp. SMS is the familiar text-message service; RCS is a newer messaging standard that supports richer exchanges. Linq also says it is an Apple Messaging Service Provider. The details of what a message can contain and how someone can interact with it vary across channels, devices, places and providers.

The company’s plans extend beyond written messages. Its computer-use offering, as Linq describes it, gives agents computing infrastructure for interacting with software and completing tasks. Its HD Calling for Agents offering adds voice calls. Together, these offerings reflect an ambition to connect the places where people express their wishes with the software that can act on them.

Linq reports having processed more than one billion messages, a cumulative figure supplied by the company. It names DoorDash, Town and Tomo among its customers and says it serves hundreds of other companies. Those figures describe the company’s reported reach and communication activity.

The clearest example of the proposed experience comes from DoorDash, the delivery business. In Linq’s press release, DoorDash’s head of AI research describes an ordering path in which customers find food, build a cart and check out inside one thread. The example comes through a named customer representative in a company announcement. It gives a concrete account of the intended flow, while its evidentiary scope remains that attributed description.

Finding food, building a cart and checking out are familiar activities. Bringing them into a conversation changes how a person sees their boundaries. A message might express a preference, request a change or approve a purchase. The system has to interpret which of those things the person means, and the person needs to understand what the system is about to do.

That brings the central question into view: when the conversation becomes the place where an action happens, how does someone see and control the moment when discussion turns into commitment?

Consider an everyday illustration: a restaurant server repeats an order before sending it to the kitchen. The repetition makes a shared understanding audible. The diner can hear the dishes, quantities and special requests together, and make a correction while the order is still being settled. Later, the receipt records what was charged.

A conversational agent could perform a similar role. Before a consequential action, it could bring together what it understood, what it proposes to do and the price or consequence. The person’s approval would then refer to that specific proposal. Afterward, a completion receipt could show the result and explain how to seek a correction or other help. This is a proposed design pattern, with details that each service would need to work out.

The value lies in making the stages easy to recognize. A person should be able to tell whether the agent is gathering preferences, presenting a proposal, awaiting approval, carrying out the action or reporting the result. Traditional apps often give those stages separate screens. A conversation can give them clear messages and interactive controls.

The timing matters as much as the wording. For a purchase, a price presented at approval helps establish what the person agreed to pay. A result returned after the action helps establish what actually happened. A receipt with the action, time, amount and support route gives the person a durable reference when they return to the thread later.

Recovery belongs to that same experience. A clarification may settle a misunderstood request. A cancellation may stop an action while the service still allows it. A correction, refund or handoff to a person may address a completed outcome. Each route depends on the task, the business and how far the action has progressed. A useful message can make the available route clear at that moment.

This also helps put Linq’s reported billion-message figure in perspective. A processed message measures communication activity. Understanding how well an agent serves people involves a different kind of evidence: whether people understood the proposed action, approved it, received the intended result and could resolve a problem. Each question concerns a stage in the task, and each calls for evidence about that stage.

The November announcement establishes Linq’s financing, its stated product direction and attributed customer examples. Assessing the experience in practice would involve examining completed tasks and their outcomes. A busy conversation and a successfully fulfilled request describe different things.

The broader possibility is appealing. A person could stay in a familiar thread while software handles the movement between services. The design challenge grows out of that convenience: the thread becomes responsible for showing the distinctions that menus, carts, checkout pages and receipts have traditionally made visible.

Different tasks will call for different treatment. An information request may need a clear answer and its source. A purchase involves money and delivery expectations. An account change affects something that persists. An irreversible action gives the approval moment particular weight.

As more of the app becomes a conversation, what must remain visible so a person can understand, approve and recover from an agent’s actions?
