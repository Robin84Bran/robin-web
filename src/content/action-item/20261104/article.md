# Who Owns a Claim When AI Writes It?

Anthropic develops Claude, a family of AI models that generate text in response to a person’s request. People can use that text in many ways, including turning a draft into an article. Developers can also build Claude into their own products through the Claude Platform. Along that journey, a passage moves between participants with different responsibilities: the company that supplies the model, the person who requests the text, and the publisher who puts it before readers.

On November 3, 2026, Anthropic announced a way to help identify text generated by certain Claude models. Its text watermark creates a statistical pattern in the model’s word choices, giving a detector a signal about where a passage likely came from.

That addresses one part of a larger problem for readers. Knowing where a sentence originated and knowing whether its claim is accurate require different kinds of evidence. Establishing who takes responsibility for correcting it adds a third question.

Think of a return address on a package. It helps identify the sender. The contents still deserve their own inspection: the item inside could be useful, damaged or different from the description. This is an analogy for the distinction between origin and accuracy. Anthropic’s actual mechanism works through patterns in generated language.

The November announcement makes that distinction concrete. A machine may help recognize the origin of a passage. What, then, helps a reader trust the claims inside it?

## What Anthropic announced

The November 3 Claude Platform release notes said that text generated by **Claude Mythos 5, Claude Opus 4.7 and Claude Sonnet 4.6** carries Anthropic’s watermark on the platforms the company lists. Those model names and the platform qualification define the announcement’s scope. Anthropic maintains the covered-model and platform list in its support documentation.

According to Anthropic’s technical documentation, the watermark works through word-choice patterns while adding zero extra tokens—the units of text a model processes—and zero hidden characters. The company also says the watermark carries neither user nor organization information and leaves meaning, quality and readability unchanged. These are Anthropic’s descriptions of its system.

The signal becomes easier to detect across more text, according to the company’s public explanation. A short passage gives the detector less material to assess. Editing and translation can also reduce detection confidence because they change the language carrying the pattern.

There is a further qualification for Claude Sonnet 4.6. Developers can adjust settings called `temperature`, `top_p` and `top_k`, which influence how the model chooses among possible words. Anthropic says using custom values for these settings can produce Sonnet 4.6 output that lacks the watermark.

For developers relying on the watermark, Anthropic’s documentation says to omit all three settings from the request. That guidance also covers values supplied by the software used to connect to Claude, such as a framework or client library. A setting introduced along the way can therefore matter to the result.

Together, these details give the signal a specific meaning: it can indicate generation origin for covered models and platforms, subject to the documented settings and detection conditions. A detector’s result needs to be read within those boundaries. An uncertain result leaves the passage’s origin uncertain; the available text and the way it was produced or changed affect what the detector can establish.

## Following one sentence back to its evidence

Now consider a factual sentence a publisher might write about the announcement:

“According to Anthropic, its text watermark adds zero hidden characters and carries neither user nor organization information.”

A reader can evaluate that sentence by opening Anthropic’s technical documentation. The documentation directly supports both parts of the claim. The attribution also tells the reader whose account they are reading: the company describing its own technology.

That evidence trail does a different job from a watermark. The watermark concerns the passage’s likely generation origin. The documentation supports the sentence’s description of the technology. The publisher’s decision to include the sentence places it within an article for which someone has editorial responsibility.

The scope matters as much as the link. If the next sentence extends the claim to every Claude output, the same source would fail to support that broader wording. The documented coverage names particular models and platforms, and Sonnet 4.6 has the settings qualification. Readers need those boundaries to understand what the evidence actually establishes.

This is why a source list alone offers only a starting point. A source supports the statements it establishes. Its authority reaches as far as its contents.

One possible editorial practice is a **claim ledger**: a record connecting each important factual statement to its supporting source, the date the source was checked, and the conditions that keep the statement accurate. For this example, the record could connect the sentence to Anthropic’s technical documentation and retain the covered-model, platform and Sonnet settings qualifications beside it.

Such a ledger would be a publisher’s own practice. Anthropic’s announced watermark supplies a generation-origin signal; the editorial record supplies the source-to-claim connection. Keeping those functions distinct helps a reader understand what each piece of evidence contributes.

## What happens when the claim changes

Publication is one moment in a claim’s life. The underlying information may change afterward.

Suppose Anthropic later revises its supported models, platform coverage or Sonnet parameter behavior. That is a hypothetical development, and it would give the publisher a reason to revisit the relevant wording. The article could then reflect the new documentation while a dated correction or update note preserves the earlier wording and explains the change.

That record would also help readers distinguish two different events. In one, the technology changed after an accurate article appeared. In the other, the article originally described the technology incorrectly. Both can produce revised text. An explanation of what changed, why and when makes the reason visible.

For media that uses AI throughout drafting and editing, this creates a practical meaning for accountability. The publisher can show which evidence supported a claim at publication and how it responded when that evidence changed or an error emerged. Readers gain a history they can examine.

Visible corrections require an editorial decision and a maintained record. They are a proposed publishing practice here, separate from Anthropic’s announced capability.

The watermark gives readers a potential clue about where words came from. A traceable source gives them a way to examine what those words assert. A dated correction shows how the publisher takes care of the assertion over time.

As generation origin becomes easier to identify, the question inside the article remains a human one: who stands behind this claim, and where will readers see that person or publisher correct it?
