# From Megawatts to Useful Compute

An AI data center turns electricity into computing work. Inside it, specialized chips train AI systems and run them when people ask questions, generate images or use other AI services. Customers pay for access to that computing power. For the businesses building these facilities, the journey begins with equipment and energy and ends with money arriving from customers.

Each stage has its own clock. A generator can be ordered while a site is being prepared. Servers can arrive before testing finishes. A customer can begin using a service before the invoice comes due. For an investor, understanding how those clocks fit together helps explain what a large infrastructure announcement might eventually earn.

Two recent stories bring that question into focus. OnePWR Solutions, which develops gas-fired power-generation platforms and related engineering and construction capability, sold a platform in September 2026. IREN Limited, which builds and operates data centers and AI Cloud infrastructure, reported rapid revenue growth in November while shifting the timetable for a larger revenue target. Together, the disclosures show why electricity capacity, working computers, revenue and cash deserve separate attention.

First, it helps to understand what a megawatt actually measures.

## A pipe and the water flowing through it

The US Energy Information Administration, the government agency that explains and tracks energy systems, distinguishes power from energy. A watt measures the rate at which electricity is supplied or used. A megawatt, abbreviated MW, equals one million watts. A megawatt-hour, or MWh, measures energy: one megawatt sustained for one hour.

Think of a water pipe. Its maximum flow rate resembles a generator’s rated power capacity. The amount of water delivered over an hour resembles energy delivered over time. A wide pipe offers the possibility of a large flow; the water supply and operating conditions determine what actually comes through.

Similarly, a constant 100MW delivered for one hour equals 100MWh before losses and operating constraints. A generator’s “nameplate capacity” is its rated maximum under specified conditions. Actual output depends on conditions such as fuel supply, maintenance and equipment performance.

Electricity then has another journey inside the data center. Cooling systems and electrical equipment consume part of the supply. The remainder powers the computing equipment. Servers need to be installed, tested and ready for customer use. Customers then need to buy the available service.

A headline capacity figure describes the beginning of that journey. The investment question concerns the whole trip.

## What the announcements establish

Piper Sandler, the investment bank that served as OnePWR’s exclusive financial adviser, records a September 2026 sale of the company’s power-generation platform for **$3.4 billion**. Its transaction record describes the buyer as a frontier AI lab—a business developing advanced AI systems. The primary record establishes the platform sale and its price; the buyer’s identity and the platform’s operating timetable remain open at that source level.

On November 5, IREN provided a different view of infrastructure progress: the movement from building capacity toward customer revenue.

The company reported **$200 million of AI Cloud revenue for the first quarter of its 2027 fiscal year**, an increase of **182% from the previous quarter**. At the same time, it moved its target for more than **$4 billion of annualized run-rate revenue** from year-end 2026 to January 2027, with a possible extension through the end of February.

An annualized run rate expresses a level of business activity on a full-year basis. Here it is a management operating target. Revenue recognized under generally accepted accounting principles, or GAAP, records revenue for a particular reporting period under accounting rules. The two figures answer different questions: one describes a targeted operating pace; the other records a period’s results.

IREN attributed the timing shift to a customer-requested design change, supplier timing, commissioning, testing and customer acceptance. Commissioning is the process of bringing equipment into service and checking that it works as intended. Customer acceptance adds a further milestone: the customer confirms that the delivered service meets the agreed requirements.

Those steps make the calendar economically important. Equipment installation, technical readiness and customer acceptance can each occur on a different date.

Three days later, on November 8, Reuters relayed Financial Times reporting that identified **Anthropic** as the buyer of OnePWR’s platform. The report described **1.7 gigawatts of gas-generation equipment commitments**, alongside engineering and construction capability needed for installation. A gigawatt equals 1,000 megawatts, making the reported figure 1,700MW.

The buyer identity and capacity figure remain attributed to that reporting. Reuters said independent verification remained outstanding and reported that requests for comment to both companies went unanswered. Piper Sandler’s record identifies the buyer only by its description as a frontier AI lab.

The reported equipment commitments concern a stage upstream of operating electricity supply. Delivery, installation, fuel arrangements and commercial operation each require their own evidence. Treating these as separate milestones preserves what the transaction tells an investor while keeping the operating possibilities open.

## Following the electricity into cash

Once electricity reaches a data center, the next useful unit is an **accelerator-hour**. An accelerator is a specialized computing chip, such as a graphics processing unit, or GPU, used for AI work. One accelerator available for one hour provides one available accelerator-hour.

Availability and customer use are separate measures. A functioning chip can be ready to work while waiting for a job. Utilization measures how much of the available capacity gets used. The portion that customers purchase, under the relevant contract terms, becomes saleable computing service.

This gives an investor a practical sequence to follow: rated equipment capacity becomes reliable electrical output; part of that output reaches computing equipment; installed and accepted accelerators provide available hours; customer use and commercial terms turn those hours into billable service; collections turn invoices into cash.

Different forces act along the sequence. Construction delays shift the start date. Outages and reduced operating output lower electricity delivery. Cooling consumes power. Server delivery and acceptance determine when computing service becomes available. Demand and pricing determine how much that service earns. Payment terms determine when the cash arrives.

These are analytical variables. Their actual values belong to each project and contract.

Cash also flows outward while a project develops. In its November 5 release, IREN described **$15 billion of remaining planned capital expenditure for the fiscal year**—spending on infrastructure and equipment. It also described approximately **$6 billion of funding still to come** from financing, customer prepayments and other sources, subject to market conditions.

That disclosure illustrates how construction schedules and funding schedules interact. A customer prepayment can bring cash forward. Borrowing carries financing costs. A later service start can move customer receipts further into the future while development spending continues. IREN’s figures describe its own plans and funding position; other projects require their own analysis.

## Giving every stage its own assumptions

This is the purpose of the proposed **AI Power Portfolio Simulator**: to explore how power and equipment could become useful computing service and collected cash across a group of investments. Its present status is a research proposal. Building and running it would be subsequent work.

A useful design would preserve each stage separately. It would accept assumptions for power availability, facility overhead, accelerator count, utilization, price per accelerator-hour, commissioning date, financing cost and collection timing. Fuel reliability, delivery schedules and customer acceptance would help determine when capacity becomes usable. Customer concentration would show how much revenue depends on a small number of buyers.

The results would be ranges of possible outcomes and a view of which assumptions change those outcomes most. For example, a scenario could explore a later commissioning date while holding customer pricing steady. Another could examine lower utilization after an on-time opening. Keeping those changes separate would make their effects easier to understand.

Any conversion from an illustrative 100MW into accelerator-hours or cash would carry its assumptions alongside it. Company disclosures would remain distinct from scenario inputs, and calculated possibilities would remain distinct from reported results.

The value of that exercise lies in making a familiar headline more informative. Two projects can each advertise 100MW while following different schedules, serving different customers and collecting cash on different terms.

When two projects both say “100MW,” how much useful compute and collected cash does each actually produce, and when?
