# Who Learned the Math After the Chat Closed?

A mathematics problem used to sit still on the page. A student could stare at it, try a calculation, rub it out and try again. With an AI tutor beside the exercise, the page becomes a conversation. The student can ask for a hint, have a line of working checked or receive instructions for the next step. Some tutors can even see handwritten calculations through a phone camera.

That creates a promising possibility: help at the moment a learner gets stuck. It also gives education researchers two things to follow. One is the work completed while help is available. The other is the skill the learner carries away.

Think of following directions through an unfamiliar town. Each instruction can help you reach your destination. Finding your own way on a later trip draws on the map you have built in your head. Both journeys matter, and each reveals something different.

Three studies of AI mathematics tutoring bring that distinction into focus. They involve adults working online, high-school students practising mathematics and middle-school students receiving daily remedial instruction. The participants, tools and measurements differ. Together, they sharpen a question that can get buried beneath a successfully completed worksheet: what can the learner do after the conversation ends?

## A tutor that can see the working

In a randomized online study described in a preprint, 117 adults worked on calculus problems. Calculus is the branch of mathematics used to study change and accumulation, such as how speed varies during a journey.

Researchers assigned participants to three forms of assistance. A text-based tutor responded to what learners typed. A visual tutor could also see their handwritten work through a phone camera and respond when asked. A third version had visual access and could take the initiative in offering help.

The practical difference is easy to appreciate. A typed question gives a tutor whatever the learner chooses to describe. A view of the page can reveal the steps already taken, including where a calculation begins to go astray.

The researchers analysed 2,140 tutor messages. Visual access shifted assistance toward checking work in progress. Proactive assistance increased error identification and directive hints—guidance telling a learner what to do next.

The tutor’s behaviour changed. The performance results were more restrained. Participants solved about six of ten problems on average, and the dated public coverage reports that performance differences among the three conditions fell short of statistical significance. In everyday terms, the results left the researchers with insufficient grounds to declare a performance advantage for one mode.

This study helps describe how a tutor’s eyes and initiative alter the conversation. A claim that proactive visual assistance produces higher learning scores remains an open hypothesis.

That distinction matters because spotting an error, helping someone finish an exercise and helping them develop an independent skill are three different achievements.

## What happened when access ended

A field experiment involving nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students followed the transition from assisted practice to independent work more directly.

The study, published in the peer-reviewed journal *PNAS*, compared two forms of GPT-4 assistance with a group that worked independently. GPT Base resembled a standard ChatGPT interface. GPT Tutor used prompts designed to safeguard learning.

While students had access to the tools, their grades improved substantially relative to the comparison group: 48% with GPT Base and 127% with GPT Tutor. These are relative improvements, rather than percentage-point additions to a grade.

Then access was removed.

Students who had used GPT Base scored 17% below the group that had practised independently throughout. The guarded GPT Tutor largely mitigated this adverse learning effect.

The sequence gives the result its meaning. During practice, the general chat-style tool helped students achieve higher grades. In the subsequent independent assessment, that same group performed worse than the comparison group. The authors interpret unrestricted GPT-4 use as a crutch during practice for some students.

That interpretation belongs to the particular tool, students and teaching setup tested. The stronger results for the guarded tutor also make the design of assistance central to the story. Two interfaces built around the same underlying AI technology supported different learning outcomes.

The educational question becomes more precise here. Which kinds of help allow a student to succeed today while building the reasoning needed for tomorrow?

## A school tool meets daily habits

A third study moves from a particular tutoring exchange to everyday school use.

An NBER working paper describes a two-year trial across 18 Tennessee middle schools. Researchers used a cluster-randomized design, assigning groups rather than individual students separately. During daily remedial mathematics sessions, students assigned to Khan Academy with Khanmigo could use the learning platform alongside its AI assistant.

Assignment to that combination raised achievement by 1.3 national percentile ranks per term. A percentile rank describes a student’s position compared with other students nationally. The paper also expresses the annual gain as approximately 0.06–0.08 standard deviations, a way researchers compare changes across different tests.

The working paper’s abstract says the gains resembled those from Khan Academy practice alone. The improvement associated with the combined offering therefore needs to be understood alongside that comparison.

Usage records offer a possible explanation. Fully 96% of students tried Khanmigo at least once. For the median student—the student in the middle of the usage distribution—messages occurred on one third of practice days and in 17% of exercise sessions involving a mistake. Messages were mostly bare answers or clicks on suggested prompts.

Trying an assistant once and having a substantial conversation about a mistake are different levels of engagement. The authors identify limited substantive use as one explanation for the modest additional effects of the AI assistance.

This adds another step to the story. A tutor can be available, a student can open it, and the resulting exchange can still be brief. The learning opportunity depends partly on what happens inside that exchange.

The three studies preserve three distinct views: how assistance changes when a tutor can see and intervene, how students perform after access ends, and how an assistant gets used across ordinary school days. Their different populations and measurements call for separate interpretations.

## Try the transition yourself

A short exercise can make the change from assisted to independent work tangible. Treat this as an editorial invitation to observe your own process.

Start with **Problem A, with a tool allowed**:

> A bicycle rental costs $12 plus $4 for each hour. The final bill is $36. How many hours was the bicycle rented? Show the equation and check the answer.

Record your time and whether you used a hint. Then close the tool before attempting **Problem B**:

> A kayak rental costs $15 plus $5 for each hour. The final bill is $50. How many hours was the kayak rented? Show the equation and check the answer.

Record the time and any hint use again. Score each attempt out of four, awarding one point for each of these:

- Writing the correct equation.
- Solving for the correct number of hours.
- Substituting the result back into the original total.
- Explaining the reasoning in one clear sentence.

For checking after both attempts: the bicycle equation is \(12 + 4h = 36\), giving six hours; \(12 + 4 \times 6 = 36\). The kayak equation is \(15 + 5h = 50\), giving seven hours; \(15 + 5 \times 7 = 50\). In each case, subtracting the starting fee leaves the hourly charges, which you divide by the hourly rate.

The pair shares a structure while changing the numbers and setting. Your two scores can prompt a useful reflection about which steps you understood and which steps the tool supplied.

Self-selected reports offer examples and counterexamples. Estimating a causal learning effect requires a randomized comparison; measuring long-term retention requires later assessments. This brief exercise captures an immediate attempt.

An AI tutor can make a difficult page feel approachable. It can catch an error and help a learner move forward. The next question is equally concrete: when a fresh problem appears and the chat has closed, which part of that help has become the learner’s own skill?
