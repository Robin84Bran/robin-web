# Google’s Geothermal Deal Has Two Different Tests

Under Central Oregon’s Newberry Volcano lies the resource Quaise Energy wants to turn into electricity: heat. Its proposed Project Obsidian, on the volcano’s southern flank, would bring underground heat to the surface and use it to generate power. Google, which operates data centers in Oregon, has agreed to buy electricity from the project’s first phase.

The partnership brings together two needs. Google has electricity demand from existing operations. Quaise, a geothermal developer and drilling-technology company, needs to turn a promising underground resource into a working power station. A long-term ambition sits behind that practical task: reaching deeper, hotter rock could make geothermal energy much more productive.

Quaise also has an unusual tool in development. Its millimeter-wave drilling system uses concentrated electromagnetic energy to remove hard rock. That technology helps explain the interest in the company. The project’s sequence, however, gives the story its shape: established drilling equipment comes first, and Quaise’s distinctive drilling system is planned for the expansion.

Understanding that sequence reveals what Google’s agreement establishes today—and what the ground still has to reveal.

## Turning underground heat into electricity

Geothermal power depends on moving heat. Fluid travels through hot rock, absorbs energy and carries it to the surface, where equipment converts that energy into electricity. In an enhanced geothermal system, developers create or improve pathways through the rock so the fluid can circulate.

Higher temperatures offer a potential advantage. Hotter fluid can carry more useful energy, allowing a well to support more electricity production. Quaise describes temperatures above 300°C as “superhot” and claims that superhot wells could deliver up to ten times the power per well of conventional geothermal. That figure is a company performance claim and a design ambition; commercial operating results will determine what its projects achieve.

Reaching those temperatures also makes the engineering harder. The US Department of Energy describes the Oregon demonstration work as targeting temperatures of 265–365°C at the bottom of the well. The upper part of that range exceeds temperatures at which most conventional enhanced-geothermal tools and materials have been validated.

A familiar comparison helps explain the challenge. Imagine building a heating system with its boiler buried deep underground. Making the boiler hotter increases the energy available, while also demanding more from the pipes, seals and equipment carrying that heat. At Project Obsidian, the surrounding rock adds another difficulty: engineers must first drill a route to the heat and establish a useful flow through it.

The resource and the machinery have to work together.

## From an exploration well to a buyer

Project Obsidian’s public development record begins before the Google announcement.

In September 2025, the Bureau of Land Management issued an environmental decision and decision record covering specified exploration work on a federal geothermal lease on land managed by the US Forest Service. The scope included surface surveys, a well pad, access work, a geothermal resource-confirmation well, storage areas and two freshwater wells.

BLM describes that decision as an interim approval step, with further drilling notices and permits to follow. Its significance is specific: it advanced the exploration work needed to learn more about the resource.

The Oregon Department of Energy’s November 2026 biennial report says drilling began during 2026 for a well supporting the proposed 50-megawatt plant. A megawatt is a measure of power—the rate at which electricity is generated or used. The state report describes Obsidian as a proposed facility with potential expansion.

Then, on 17 November 2026, Quaise announced that Google had signed a power-purchase agreement for 50 megawatts from the project. Such an agreement, often called a PPA, sets terms under which a buyer purchases a project’s electricity. Quaise says purchases could begin as early as 2030 and would support Google’s existing data-center operations in The Dalles and regional communities.

Oregon Public Broadcasting also reported the agreement, saying both companies had described it to the broadcaster. Quaise’s announcement includes a statement attributed to Lucia Tian, Google’s Head of Advanced Energy Technologies.

The public record therefore brings together field activity, an initial exploration decision and a named electricity buyer. Commercial generation remains a future stage, with 2030 presented as Quaise’s earliest delivery target.

## Why the first 50 megawatts matter

An electricity project needs someone willing to buy its output. Google’s agreement gives Obsidian a commercial customer for its initial phase and can help Quaise make the case for further development and financing.

The precise strength of that support depends on the contract. Its price, duration and development-risk provisions remain undisclosed in the reviewed sources. Those terms govern matters such as revenue, delivery obligations and the consequences of delays. The announcement establishes the buyer and the basic size of the deal; the financial commitments are less visible.

The crucial engineering detail is clearer. Quaise says the first 50-megawatt phase will use conventional drilling.

That makes the initial phase a substantial test in its own right. Developers must confirm the underground resource, construct wells that survive the heat, establish dependable fluid circulation and build a plant that supplies electricity. Sustained operation would show how the wells and generating equipment perform together over time.

The practical questions are straightforward. How much electricity reaches the grid after the plant uses power for its own equipment? How consistently does it run? How do the wells’ output and maintenance needs change? Those answers would turn a proposed generating capacity into a record of useful electricity delivered.

For Quaise, successful first-phase operation could establish an important commercial foundation. The evidence would belong to a superhot geothermal project built with conventional drilling. Its new drilling system has a separate assignment.

## The deeper expansion

Quaise’s project website describes a completed 250-megawatt plant developed in phases. After the initial 50 megawatts, the proposed second phase would add 200 megawatts by accessing deeper, hotter rock beneath the first wells, reaching temperatures up to 850°F, or about 454°C.

Quaise expects that expansion to become the first commercial use of its millimeter-wave drilling technology.

The system combines two approaches. Ordinary rotary drill bits handle shallower sedimentary layers. For hard basement rock, a machine at the surface called a gyrotron generates concentrated electromagnetic energy. A metallic guide carries that energy down the hole, where it melts or removes rock. Circulating gas brings the resulting fine material upward.

The attraction is easy to understand: hard rock and extreme heat put demanding conditions around conventional drilling tools. An energy-based method offers another way to attack the rock.

The field test extends beyond the speed of making a hole. A useful well needs the right shape and condition, dependable equipment and a manageable total cost. Energy consumption, maintenance and downtime all affect that result.

DOE’s account of geothermal drilling adds a particularly concrete detail: casing and cement—the materials that line and help secure a well—can account for as much as half its cost. Faster rock removal addresses one part of the bill. The full economics emerge from completing a durable, productive well.

That is the question phase two would bring into focus: can Quaise’s millimeter-wave system deliver those deeper wells reliably and at an attractive total cost?

Google’s agreement gives the first phase a serious buyer. The exploration well is helping establish what lies underground. A working 50-megawatt plant would supply the next kind of evidence: electricity delivered over time. The deeper expansion would then test the drilling technology behind Quaise’s broader ambition.

Each stage has a distinct job. For now, Obsidian’s story is the effort to connect an underground heat resource to a real customer—and to earn, through that first project, the foundation for going deeper.
