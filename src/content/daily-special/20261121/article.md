# The Two Clocks Behind Faster Grid Connections

A data center needs a building full of computers and a steady supply of electricity. Putting those pieces together means coordinating two very different kinds of construction. The building can rise while the power system needed to serve it is still moving through engineering studies, permits and equipment orders. According to PJM Interconnection, data centers can be built in roughly 12–18 months, faster than the generation and transmission facilities needed to supply them.

PJM operates where those schedules meet. It coordinates the wholesale electricity market and high-voltage grid across all or parts of 13 US states and the District of Columbia. When developers propose new generators or large electricity users seek service, PJM studies how they would fit into the shared system. Adding a large customer can require stronger transmission lines, new substations or other work to keep electricity flowing reliably.

That makes the engineering study an important early step. It helps establish what work a project would require, what that work might cost and which connection or service rights could be offered. Developers need those answers to decide whether their plans make financial sense.

On November 16, 2026, PJM proposed changing how it produces those answers. Its Fast Energization Process would combine studies of proposed generation connections with transmission-service requests for eligible and large electricity users in a single process targeted at 12 months. PJM described the plan as cutting interconnection processing time in half.

The proposal went to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, or FERC, which reviews PJM’s tariff—the rules governing its services. As of November 21, it remained under Commission review. PJM requested a 60-day comment period and a decision within 120 days.

The name Fast Energization naturally draws attention to the moment electricity starts flowing. The change PJM can directly influence is the speed of its planning work. That raises the central question: how much does a faster engineering answer change the journey to an operating project?

## The study clock

There are two clocks to follow: the study clock and the steel-and-concrete clock.

Think of an engineering survey before a building project. The survey can reveal that the foundations need strengthening, estimate the cost and help the owner decide whether to proceed. Finishing it gives the construction team a clearer job to do. The foundations still require workers, materials and time.

A grid study serves a similar purpose. It maps the consequences of adding electricity supply or demand to an interconnected system. Physical delivery then depends on generation, substations, transmission lines, equipment, permits, construction and commissioning—the checks needed to put completed facilities into service.

PJM proposes shortening the study clock by replacing three full System Impact Study phases with an approximately four-month screening phase followed by one System Impact Study. The screening phase would contain two rounds of planning-level analysis. PJM says each round would produce preliminary, good-faith estimates of needed reinforcements, construction duration and costs within roughly 60–90 days. Those estimates would guide decisions while remaining subject to revision.

The formal 12-month target also sits inside a longer preparation process. Before the application deadline, PJM proposes a Soft Close Date and 14 weeks of Early Application Engagement. During that period, it would check technical models, clarify project locations and help applicants correct their submissions.

PJM says only 5% of applications in its current process arrive correct and complete on the first submission. Earlier engagement is intended to improve the information entering the formal study. The one-year target therefore describes a defined engineering process; preparation occupies its own place on the calendar.

## Earlier answers bring earlier commitments

The proposed screening rounds lead to two decision points. Applicants would receive information, decide whether to continue and commit readiness funds. Speed would come with a stronger expectation that projects arrive prepared to proceed.

For large electricity users, readiness includes relationships and agreements with the companies serving them. The filing defines a Large Load as at least 50 megawatts of peak demand at one electrical site, including certain affiliated facilities within one mile. A megawatt is a unit of power equal to one million watts; peak demand is the highest rate at which a customer draws electricity.

Eligible customers seeking transmission service for such a load would need authorization from the relevant transmission owner or electric distributor, along with an executed Transmission Owner Cost Recovery Agreement. These requirements put commercial preparation near the entrance to the process.

Generation developers would face a specific financial schedule. Under the proposed tariff, they would post an initial Readiness Deposit of $10,000 for every megawatt requested with their application. For scale, a hypothetical 100-megawatt generation project would post $1 million.

At the first decision point, the amount at risk would be $1,500 per megawatt plus 35% of the developer’s share of identified reinforcement costs, capped by the initial deposit. At the second, the required amount at risk would rise to the greater of the initial deposit or the developer’s full share of reinforcement and developer-interconnection-facility costs. That schedule applies to generation developers; large electricity customers have their own applicable requirements.

The proposal would also make physical project choices consequential earlier. A physical change to a project or load after application would require withdrawal and entry in the next cycle. PJM’s explanation is practical: a compressed study of a group of projects leaves the models tightly linked, with the available time committed to studying the submitted designs.

The capital implication follows from that sequence. Earlier engineering information could help developers assess a site sooner. At the same time, they would need firmer technical plans, counterparties, agreements and funds earlier. A shorter wait for an answer could also mean an earlier moment when continuing becomes expensive.

Whether that combination helps clear speculative projects from the process is an open question. The proposed rules establish the mechanism; future cycles would show how developers respond.

## The steel and concrete clock

The transition itself would take time. PJM expects the first submission window to open in late November 2027, followed by a Soft Close Date in early May 2028 and an application deadline in August 2028. It expects the first cycle to finish in 2029.

Those are proposed dates. The start depends on sufficient progress in Cycle 01 of PJM’s existing process. Although some tariff sections carry a proposed March 16, 2027 effective date, the Fast Energization provisions have an indefinite effective date pending that transition.

Beyond the study, the delivery clock keeps running on its own requirements. PJM’s filing describes projects with signed agreements that remain unbuilt or face delays from state permitting and supply-chain backlogs. A completed study can clarify the work ahead while equipment deliveries, construction and financing continue to determine a project’s service date.

The proposal does include a possible route to some earlier service. It would offer conditional interim rights to put electricity onto the grid or draw electricity from it, based on the grid’s existing configuration. PJM would reassess those rights annually as conditions change. Their value would depend on the service available to a particular project and the conditions attached to it; full requested service would retain its separate requirements.

A faster answer can still matter. It can reveal an expensive reinforcement earlier, give a promising project a clearer basis for proceeding or bring a withdrawal forward before more resources accumulate around the plan. Those are potential benefits of better-timed information, alongside the physical work needed to deliver electricity.

The first completed cycle will give that possibility a measurable shape: which projects withdraw, how closely early cost estimates match later requirements, and how much time passes between study completion and actual service. Together, those results will show how the two clocks moved.
