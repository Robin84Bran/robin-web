# When Does the AI Factory Clock Start?

Every time an AI model answers a question, some computing equipment has to do the work. The model’s training gave it its capabilities; using those capabilities to answer a fresh request is called *inference*. Behind that answer sit processors, electricity, cooling and a network connection.

Getting those pieces ready together is a substantial part of bringing AI capacity into service. A powerful computer needs a suitable place to operate. Its electricity supply must support its demand, its cooling must carry away heat, and its network must connect it to customers. A delivery schedule therefore stretches across several kinds of work, each with its own definition of “ready.”

Torus, which describes itself as a U.S. manufacturer of power and computing infrastructure, wants to bring those pieces together before they arrive at a customer’s site. Its new product, Foundry, packages power, energy storage, cooling, networking and computing equipment in a prefabricated system.

On November 11, 2026, Torus launched Foundry with a striking promise: “from contract to running AI workloads in 12–16 weeks.”

That sounds like a stopwatch. Understanding the promise means understanding exactly when the stopwatch starts—and what a customer receives when it stops.

Torus calls the journey “contract to token.” A token is a small unit of text or data processed by an AI model. In this phrase, it serves as shorthand for an AI workload running on the equipment. How often that equipment serves requests, and how much income it produces, are separate questions. The first successful answer is one milestone; a busy service earning money over time is another.

The participants also occupy different places along that journey. Torus supplies infrastructure. An operator can make its computing capacity available to buyers. Those buyers then use the capacity to run their workloads.

Hydra Host, one of the initial customers named by Torus, helps explain that middle layer. In a June 15, 2026 announcement, Hydra Host described Brokkr, its software for deploying, managing and selling capacity from GPUs—the processors widely used for AI work. It said Brokkr operated across more than 50 data centers and connected infrastructure owners with buyers seeking dedicated GPU capacity.

That description suggests how Hydra Host could participate as an operator or a link between infrastructure owners and customers. Its particular Foundry arrangement remains unspecified in the reviewed sources. At the November 12 research cutoff, Torus’s announcement supplied the public evidence reviewed for the new relationship.

Torus also identified a second initial customer as an AI cloud provider whose name it kept private. Alongside the product launch, it announced $500 million in growth capital led by Magnetar. The funding announcement gives a sense of the company’s ambitions; the delivery promise concerns a more concrete unit of progress: a customer’s infrastructure becoming usable.

Foundry’s proposed route to that result is easy to grasp. Move more work into the factory, then bring an assembled and tested system to the site.

On its product page, Torus describes a standard building block containing 576 GPUs, with eight server racks and four networking racks in a 577-square-foot system footprint. Its stated computing-equipment power load is 1.2 megawatts, or 1,200 kilowatts. These figures describe the product configuration Torus is offering. The announcement reports the existing fleet separately, in aggregate.

Torus says site preparation takes about a week and installation and commissioning take one to two days. Commissioning is the process of checking that the installed equipment works together and is ready for service. The company says individual sites can range from 1 to 20 megawatts.

Those short on-site stages help explain the appeal. Consider a kitchen prepared in a factory. Cabinets arrive assembled, with much of the fitting already done. That preparation can shorten the work inside the house considerably. The household’s full wait still depends on the starting point: signing the order, having the room ready, or receiving the delivery.

The kitchen is an illustration, and Foundry’s equipment is more complex. The timing principle carries across. A system that arrives largely finished can genuinely shorten installation while making earlier preparation more important to the overall schedule.

Electricity is a central part of that preparation. Torus says Foundry uses existing connections to the power grid and pairs each deployment with Torus Reserve, its energy-storage and management product. Storage can charge during one period and discharge during another, helping shift demand over time and reduce peaks in the draw from the grid.

That offers a plausible way to fit computing equipment into an existing power connection. The amount of spare capacity at a particular site, and the pattern of electricity demand there, would determine how the arrangement works in practice. The short delivery path therefore rests partly on matching the system to a suitable location.

This is where two phrases in Torus’s public materials deserve close attention.

The November 11 launch announcement says “contract to token.” The Foundry product page, reviewed on November 12, says “12-16 week dirt-to-token.”

“Contract” points toward a commercial agreement. “Dirt” points toward work at the site. The reviewed pages leave the relationship between those starting events open. They could coincide in a particular project, or site preparation could begin after earlier contractual and planning work.

Either starting point could support a useful delivery measure. Naming it precisely would allow customers to understand how much of their own journey the twelve-to-sixteen-week period covers.

The finish line also depends on what the customer buys. Torus describes three commercial models. Its Strategic offering provides a powered shell, with GPUs supplied separately. Partner includes power, enclosure and GPUs. Direct offers inference capacity dedicated to a single customer.

Each arrangement gives “token” a different commercial meaning. For a powered-shell buyer, readiness of the facility is a milestone on the way to running its own equipment. For a buyer of inference capacity, a successful workload may sit much closer to the service being purchased. A shared measure needs to identify which of those outcomes counts as delivery.

Torus says Foundry already has “thousands of GPUs deployed today,” and it plans to have more than 47,000 GPUs in Foundry units by the end of 2027. The first statement is the company’s report of its current fleet; the second is its expansion plan.

The fleet claim and the delivery claim answer different questions. One describes the reported scale of equipment in place. The other describes elapsed time for a customer’s journey. Connecting them would require a dated account of the same deployment from its agreed starting event through its agreed finish.

As of November 12, 2026, at 09:42 Hong Kong time, the reviewed disclosures supported a product design, Torus’s aggregate deployment claim and its stated delivery benchmark. Customer-level dates remained unpublished. The twelve-to-sixteen-week figure consequently stands as Torus’s benchmark, with independent calculation awaiting a published start and finish for an identifiable deployment.

A compact customer example could make the promise tangible: an agreed contract milestone on one date, an accepted customer workload on another, and confirmation that both belong to the same installation. That would turn a memorable phrase into an interval a reader could calculate.

Foundry’s story is about bringing more of an AI facility to a site already assembled. Its next revealing chapter could be much smaller than the launch announcement: a customer’s dated delivery receipt, showing exactly what became ready and when.
