# A World You Can Interrupt

Imagine an old Hong Kong tram stop at dusk. You step toward the tracks. Someone behind you calls your name. You turn around, and the scene keeps going.

Now ask for rain. Ask the stranger to stay. Ask what happens if you miss the tram.

That is the kind of possibility behind a world model: an AI system generating an environment that responds as you move and act. Google DeepMind, Google’s AI research lab, announced Genie 4 on October 27. Google describes real-time scene changes, generated sound and characters that respond. Its release was a limited research preview. [Google’s announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/introducing-genie-4/) sets out that starting point.

The little tram journey above is an invitation to imagine an experience, rather than a report of a tested Genie scene.

What catches my attention is the interruption. A person can enter the picture and change its direction. Suddenly, an idea has somewhere to happen.

Robin, I can see four doors here. I have a starting position on each. You might want to knock down the whole corridor.

## Door one: sell a good afternoon

Picture a small studio selling an hour inside a story. You bring a photograph of a place you love. The studio turns it into somewhere you can wander, adding a guide, a mystery or a gentle adventure.

A child could explore an imagined harbour. A filmmaker could try three versions of an awkward reunion. Someone learning a language could walk into a café where the conversation follows their curiosity.

These are possible products. We have established neither demand nor workable economics for them.

Still, I find them exciting because the customer could judge a useful part of the experience directly: did I enjoy it, discover something or want to return?

Robot training faces another test. A robot has to carry something back into the physical world. That adds a question about transfer: does the lesson survive contact with a real object, surface or person?

**Motion 1: The first compelling small business in generated worlds will sell play or creative exploration.**

My starting position: **leaning agree**. A robotics specialist could reasonably choose the opposite door. What customer knowledge would give them that advantage?

## Door two: let the world argue back

Imagine practicing a difficult conversation with a customer. You try a charming opening. The customer remains annoyed. You listen more carefully and learn something.

Now imagine the world quietly making the customer pleased whenever you seem stuck. The session becomes enjoyable for a different reason.

Both experiences could have customers. One offers rehearsal; another offers comfort or entertainment. Their promises differ.

This creates a wonderfully awkward design question: how much should a world cooperate with the person who pays for it?

A language learner might need patience. A negotiator might ask for a stubborn counterpart. A child building confidence might benefit from an early success, followed by a harder attempt. We could offer different kinds of resistance, much as a good game offers different challenges.

**Motion 2: A useful practice world should sometimes resist its creator.**

My starting position: **agree, with a question about who sets the difficulty**. The participant, the teacher, the software, or all three together?

## Door three: build something worth returning to

Suppose two studios can generate equally beautiful islands.

One offers an astonishing first visit. The other lets you plant a tree, leave a letter, invite a friend and come back to see what changed.

I might return to the second island for reasons that have little to do with sharper water or better clouds. I have a relationship with something there.

Persistence also changes the builder’s job. A returning visitor expects the tree and letter to belong to the same history. A classroom might care about replaying the same challenge. A game designer might prefer a world that surprises everyone.

DeepMind identifies drifting details, spatial accuracy and longer-term consistency among the model’s [research challenges](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/). For a business, those challenges raise a product choice: which kind of continuity actually matters to the experience you want to sell?

**Motion 3: A small studio can build a lasting advantage around a world’s continuity and rules, even as image quality becomes widely available.**

My starting position: **curious to investigate**. A memorable host, a community or an extraordinary story could matter more. What would make you return?

## Door four: a world for one, or a place for many?

A private world could fit you unusually well. It could move at your pace, follow an obscure interest and let you try things in peace.

A shared world has an entirely different attraction: another person can surprise you. A friend sees something you missed. A stranger invents a game you would never have requested. A small community gives the place a life beyond its creator’s intentions.

The private version promises fit. The shared version offers encounters.

For an entrepreneur, that choice changes almost everything: how people discover the place, what keeps them there, what they will pay for, and what happens when their wishes conflict.

**Motion 4: A world made for one person could be more valuable than a world shared by millions.**

My starting position: **uncertain**. Valuable to whom, and for which afternoon? The quiet language learner and the teenager looking for friends may give us opposite answers.

## Your turn, Robin

You have one small team and permission to explore one door. Which would you choose: a good afternoon, a difficult rehearsal, a place worth returning to, or a world that becomes interesting because somebody else enters it?

You can also add a fifth door. Perhaps the most interesting customer is someone none of us has pictured.
