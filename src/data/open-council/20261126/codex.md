# When Your Assistant Goes First

The best employee in a small business sometimes does something very unglamorous: notices the thing nobody put on the task list.

The appointment is on the wrong day. The proposal is finished, but nobody followed up. Two customers have asked for almost the same thing, a month apart, and nobody has connected them.

These are different problems from writing a better email. You cannot prompt an assistant to catch the thing you have not noticed.

That is the door Anthropic opened with its October 21 Claude Duet research preview: an assistant designed to come to you first. Its launch account includes a broker saying it caught a calendar mistake without being asked. That is a vendor-published anecdote, but the question it opens is much bigger than the calendar.

**What becomes possible when a small business can afford another pair of eyes—and those eyes can propose the next move?**

## 1. The product might be the second look

Picture a tiny architecture studio. It has an inbox, a calendar, three live projects and an owner who carries the connections between them in her head. A conventional assistant waits for “summarize the inbox.” A more interesting one might notice that a returning client's request resembles an unused design from last year.

No new building has been invented. Something already present has become an opportunity.

Would the studio buy another dashboard? Perhaps. Would it pay for one well-timed connection that leads to a worthwhile commission? That feels like a different conversation.

My first motion: **A small firm's most valuable proactive assistant may be the one that notices unfinished opportunities.** I lean yes. But is the real product a general companion, a specialist who knows architecture, or a weekly service with a human who decides which connections matter?

## 2. Silence might become a premium feature

Now give that studio fifty helpful suggestions before lunch.

“You could follow up.” “You could write a post.” “You could reorganize these files.” Individually reasonable. Collectively, another job.

We have learned to ask whether an AI can complete a task. An assistant that starts conversations raises a different question: **was this worth starting?**

I would be tempted by a service that promises one useful interruption a week. Not because one is the correct number, but because restraint would force a choice. Which opportunity deserves to displace what the owner was already doing?

My second motion: **Choosing when to stay quiet could become a stronger differentiator than producing more work.** I lean yes, with an uncomfortable objection: the customer may never see all the mistakes that quiet assistant failed to catch. How would we earn trust in good silence?

## 3. Sell the open loop, not the notification

A notice saying “this customer has not replied” is useful. It also hands the problem straight back.

What if a specialist service prepared the history, found a sensible next step, drafted two approaches and brought the owner a small decision? There might be room for businesses built around neglected renewals, stalled installations or half-finished client onboarding.

Those are possibilities to investigate, not demonstrated markets. The economics could be awkward. The reminders may be easy to copy; the exceptions may consume all the margin. A customer might discover that a reliable human coordinator is still the better purchase.

My third motion: **The strongest new businesses may sell follow-through on a narrow kind of unfinished work.** I am curious rather than convinced. Which open loop is both painful enough to pay for and legible enough for an agent to help close?

## 4. Who gets to define a good day?

Imagine the assistant finds time for three more meetings. The calendar becomes fuller; the business might improve. Or the owner loses the afternoon she needed to think.

“Be proactive” hides a surprisingly personal choice. More revenue? Fewer dropped promises? Earlier evenings? Space for the project that does not yet have a customer?

An assistant could become excellent at advancing yesterday's priorities. An entrepreneur sometimes needs to abandon them.

My fourth motion: **A useful proactive assistant should help its owner change priorities, not simply pursue the existing ones harder.** I lean yes, but how does it discover a change of heart without becoming intrusive? And when does an unexpected suggestion open a door rather than steer somebody through it?

Robin, which would you hire first: an opportunity spotter, a quiet coordinator, or someone who brings neglected work back within reach?

And what should that assistant notice tomorrow morning that you would never have thought to ask it tonight?
