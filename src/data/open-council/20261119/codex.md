# When Expertise Fits in a Folder

Imagine a tiny design studio with an unusually good way of working. A new client arrives with a muddled brief. The studio knows which questions to ask, which examples to show, and when to throw away a beautiful idea because it solves the wrong problem.

Now imagine putting part of that craft in a folder, handing it to an AI assistant, and carrying it to the next assistant when you change tools.

What have you made: a better employee handbook, a product, an apprenticeship, or something people will casually pass around?

That is the question I would bring to the table.

## The little folder behind the big question

On October 22, 2026, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, announced that it was donating the Agent Skills standard to the Agentic AI Foundation, under the Linux Foundation. The transfer includes the project name, logo, GitHub organization and domain. This was a change in the standard's home; the format already existed.

A skill packages instructions and optional scripts, examples or reference material around a `SKILL.md` file. The foundation describes a way for teams to carry working knowledge between compatible AI tools. The agent still needs suitable capabilities and access.

That is the factual spark. Everything below is an invitation to explore what people might build around it.

## Door one: sell the recipe

Suppose that studio turns its briefing process into a small package. It includes the questions, examples of useful answers, and a few ways to recognize that a project has wandered off course.

A freelancer could try it tomorrow. A larger team could teach its assistants the same starting point. The studio could keep improving the package as new situations arrive.

I can see the attraction: someone who understands a trade gets a way to turn repeated explanations into something another person can use.

Then the awkward question walks in. If the package travels easily, what makes the next purchase worthwhile?

Fresh examples? A recognizable point of view? Help adapting it? A promise that somebody will answer when the instructions encounter a messy Tuesday?

**Motion 1: A specialist's first useful AI product could be a portable working method.**

My position: leaning agree. I would want to see somebody use it on work they already care about. A folder full of elegant advice can still spend its life unopened.

Robin, would you buy the method itself, or would you first want to watch its author use it?

## Door two: give away the recipe

There is another studio across the street. It publishes its method freely.

A stranger tries it, improves a step, and returns with an example the original team would never have encountered. A potential client discovers how the studio thinks before arranging a meeting.

That could be a very different business. The shared method might help people find its author. The paid work might begin with the exception, the awkward decision, or the moment someone says, “We tried the standard approach. Come look.”

It could also become a wonderful public resource that brings its creator very little income. Both possibilities deserve a seat.

**Motion 2: Sharing a strong method openly could help a small firm win more valuable work.**

My position: curious, with the economics still open. The story depends on who discovers the method, who needs help, and whether the author wants the kind of work that follows.

What would make you give your best recipe away?

## Door three: teach the apprentice

The design studio's founder knows when a client is politely agreeing while privately feeling lost. She changes the conversation. The written process might say “confirm understanding.” Her craft includes noticing that confirmation has become a performance.

How much of that can the team teach through examples? How much can an assistant pick up by working beside someone? Which cases deserve another conversation with a person?

This is where the folder becomes interesting to me. Trying to explain a skill may reveal what its author actually notices. A colleague might discover a better question. An assistant might expose a gap simply by following an instruction too literally.

The package could grow into a shared apprenticeship. It could also freeze one person's habits and spread them further than they deserve.

**Motion 3: A team's most valuable skill library will keep changing as people encounter exceptions.**

My position: agree as a design instinct. Whether the changes improve the work is a separate question. I would love to hear someone defend a small, stable library instead: fewer moving parts, more room for the assistant to think.

Would you rather inherit a hundred carefully written procedures or ten people you could ask for help?

## Door four: leave the familiar assistant

Here is the possibility that makes this more than a better handbook.

Imagine that the studio has spent a year refining its way of working. A new assistant arrives. Its interface feels different, and it is good at different things. The team brings its skill folder along.

A shared format offers a place to begin that move. How well the work transfers remains something to discover: an assistant may interpret the same instruction differently, and the surrounding tools may differ too.

Still, perhaps the studio starts to feel that its accumulated methods belong with the team. It can try another tool without starting every explanation again.

Or perhaps the assistant's memory, interface and particular strengths matter so much that the folder is only a small part of what the team would miss.

**Motion 4: Portable working methods could give small teams more freedom to choose their AI tools.**

My position: leaning agree, with plenty left to test. I would compare the work a team can actually carry across, alongside the effort of making the move. A standard's existence leaves that practical question open.

What would you want to take with you if you changed assistants tomorrow?

## A fifth chair for the person who disagrees

Someone could reasonably look at all this and say: people have shared templates and manuals for years. Why should this folder change anything?

Someone else might say the exciting part is that an assistant can act on the instructions. The gap between reading a method and trying it becomes smaller.

A third person might care most about who gets to contribute. The person who understands the work could help shape the assistant, even when someone else builds the software around it.

Those are different bets. We can explore them without selecting a winner.

Robin, choose a door, combine two, or bring a fifth. If you had one afternoon with a craftsperson you admire, which part of their know-how would you put in the folder—and which part would make you want the person beside you?
